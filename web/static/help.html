Introduction

Forensic Similarity Intelligence System

FSIS is a tool for comparing the chemical profiles of drug samples to identify those that may share a common origin — such as the same clandestine laboratory, synthesis batch, or supply chain. It does this by computing similarity scores between samples based on the substances detected and their proportions.

A high similarity score between two samples means their chemical profiles are closely matched. This does not prove a common origin on its own, but provides an objective, quantitative basis for prioritising samples for further investigation.

Forensic caveat FSIS provides triage and intelligence support. All findings should be considered alongside case context, analytical uncertainty, and expert interpretation before any evidential conclusions are drawn.
Similarity Metrics

How Similarity is Measured

Each sample is represented as a vector of substance percentages — one value per substance detected across the dataset. FSIS computes four independent similarity scores for every pair of samples, then combines them into a single Joint Similarity score.

Cosine Similarity

Profile Shape

Cosine similarity measures the angle between two sample vectors, capturing whether the overall shape of the chemical profiles match — regardless of absolute concentrations.

Two samples with the same proportional profile (e.g. 90% cocaine, 5% levamisole, 5% caffeine) will score close to 1.0 even if the total recovered amounts differ. This makes it robust to variation in sample size or recovery efficiency.

cos(A, B) = (A · B) / (‖A‖ × ‖B‖)

When it is most useful: Detecting samples with a similar composition pattern even when concentrations vary slightly between seizures.

Jaccard Similarity

Substance Overlap

Jaccard similarity compares which substances are present or absent in each sample, ignoring how much of each substance is present. It answers the question: what fraction of the combined substance set do both samples share?

J(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|

A score of 1.0 means both samples contain exactly the same set of substances. A score of 0 means they share none.

When it is most useful: Identifying samples that share an unusual combination of trace substances (e.g. a rare adulterant present in both), which may be more forensically significant than proportional similarity.

Euclidean Similarity

Proportional Closeness

Euclidean similarity converts the straight-line distance between two sample vectors into a similarity score. It is sensitive to differences in the actual proportions of substances, not just their presence or profile shape.

euc_sim(A, B) = 1 / (1 + ‖A − B‖)

Two samples where cocaine is 90% vs 70% will score lower here than under cosine similarity. This makes Euclidean similarity stricter and more sensitive to formulation differences.

When it is most useful: Tightening comparisons to only flag samples with very similar concentrations — useful where production consistency is expected within a batch.

Both samples contain exactly the same 7 substances — Jaccard = 1.0. Yet the profiles are dramatically different: sample 516 is 69% Heroin with Caffeine as a secondary component, while C054 is 62% Caffeine with only a trace of Heroin (0.1%). These samples may have been cut to very different purity levels, or may represent different stages in the supply chain.

The Euclidean score of 0.538 reflects this large proportional gap. The Cosine score of 0.237 also flags the very different profile shapes. Jaccard alone would suggest a strong match; Cosine and Euclidean reveal that the actual formulations are very different. This is why FSIS combines all three metrics rather than relying on any one alone.

Compare this with C056 vs C059 (Jaccard example above): also Jaccard = 1.0, but with near-identical proportions — Euclidean = 0.979. The Euclidean score distinguishes these two very different situations.

TF-IDF Similarity

Rare Substance Weighting

TF-IDF (Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency) is adapted from text analysis. It up-weights substances that are rare across the dataset and down-weights ubiquitous ones.

For example, in a cocaine dataset, cocaine itself appears in almost every sample and carries little discriminating power. A specific impurity appearing in only a handful of samples is far more informative — TF-IDF amplifies this signal.

TF-IDF(s, t) = percentage(s,t) × log((1+N) / (1+df(t))) + 1

where N = total samples, df(t) = samples containing substance t

Similarity is then the cosine similarity of the TF-IDF weighted vectors.

When it is most useful: Linking samples via unusual trace substances or manufacturing impurities that would otherwise be diluted by the dominant active ingredient.

Real example — Heroin dataset: why common substances are downweighted

Across the 56 Heroin samples in this dataset, Caffeine appears in 43 samples (77%) and Paracetamol in 38 (68%). Because they are so widespread, TF-IDF assigns them a low weight — knowing that two samples both contain Caffeine tells you very little about whether they share a common origin.

By contrast, Hydrocotarnine appears in only 9 samples (16%) and Noscapine in 6 (11%). These are opium-alkaloid impurities that are characteristic of specific raw material sources or processing methods. When two samples both contain Noscapine, that is a much more informative signal — TF-IDF amplifies it accordingly.

In the near-perfect pair C056 vs C059, both samples contain Noscapine (0.3%), Hydrocotarnine (~0.6%), and Morphine (~0.1%) in addition to their dominant components. Under standard cosine similarity, these trace substances contribute little because their absolute values are tiny. TF-IDF up-weights them, rewarding the shared rare profile and strengthening the linkage signal.

Pearson Similarity

Profile Pattern Similarity

Pearson similarity measures whether two samples share the same relative pattern of substances, regardless of their overall concentrations. It is mathematically equivalent to cosine similarity computed on mean-centred rows — each sample's average substance value is subtracted before comparison.

Where cosine asks "do these two profiles point in the same direction?", Pearson asks "do the peaks and troughs rise and fall together?". A sample that is twice as concentrated as another but has the same proportional makeup will score very high on Pearson even if Euclidean similarity is lower.

Pearson(A, B) = cosine(A − mean(A), B − mean(B))

Negative correlations (anti-patterns) are clipped to 0 — they indicate dissimilarity, not similarity.

When it is most useful: Detecting samples that share a characteristic impurity or adulterant pattern even when absolute concentrations differ between seizures or across time. Research on cocaine profiling (Morelato 2015) found Pearson to be among the best-performing single metrics for within-source discrimination.

Default weight is 0. Add weight to this metric when you want to prioritise pattern matching over absolute composition. It complements TF-IDF: TF-IDF rewards rare shared substances, while Pearson rewards a consistent relative profile across all substances.

Pearson vs Cosine — why centering matters

Imagine two cocaine samples: Sample A has 80% cocaine, 10% levamisole, 5% phenacetin, and 5% traces. Sample B has 60% cocaine, 8% levamisole, 4% phenacetin, and 28% traces from a different cutting agent. Their cosine similarity is moderate because the overall vectors point in somewhat different directions.

But after mean-centring, Pearson recognises that levamisole and phenacetin both sit above the mean in both samples, and cocaine sits above the mean in both — the relative profile pattern is consistent. Pearson captures this shared structure even though the absolute proportions differ.

This is particularly valuable when comparing seizures made at different points in a supply chain where dilution may have occurred between the source and the street sample.

√ Square Root Pre-treatment

Compressing Dominant Peaks

Square root pre-treatment applies √x to every substance value in the feature matrix before any similarity metric is computed. The transformed rows are then re-normalised to proportions so the computation is consistent with the standard pipeline.

Drug samples are often dominated by one substance — cocaine in a cocaine seizure, heroin in a heroin seizure. Without pre-treatment, these dominant peaks drive the similarity scores so strongly that minor impurities and adulterants (which carry the most forensic discrimination) contribute very little. Square root compression pulls the dominant peak down and lifts the minor components up, giving them proportionally more weight in all metrics.

x′ = √x  →  normalise row to sum to 1

Effect on each metric: All five metrics (Cosine, Jaccard, Euclidean, TF-IDF, Pearson) are recomputed from the transformed matrix when this option is enabled. Precomputed matrices loaded from disk are not used — everything is calculated live.

When to use it: Validated in published forensic drug profiling research (Morelato 2015, Michelot 2017) as improving within-source discrimination, particularly for heroin where opium-alkaloid impurities are highly informative but present at low concentrations. A good starting point when the dominant active ingredient is washing out links that you expect to see.

When not to use it: If the concentration ratios between substances are themselves the forensic signal (e.g. a precise cutting ratio is the link), sqrt compression reduces that signal. Use standard mode for batch-matching investigations where absolute proportions matter.

Example — Heroin: before and after √ pre-treatment

A sample might be 72% heroin, 15% caffeine, 8% paracetamol, and 5% across several trace impurities including noscapine (0.4%) and hydrocotarnine (0.6%). Under standard normalisation, those two trace impurities together represent only 1% of the vector — their contribution to cosine similarity is tiny.

After √ pre-treatment: √0.72 ≈ 0.85, √0.004 ≈ 0.063, √0.006 ≈ 0.077. After re-normalisation, noscapine and hydrocotarnine now represent a larger share of the transformed vector. Two samples that share these impurities will score noticeably higher — the forensic signal is amplified without discarding any data.

Joint Similarity Score

Combined Score

The five metrics are combined into a single Joint Similarity score using a geometric (multiplicative) fusion. This is more conservative than averaging — if any one metric is weak, the joint score is pulled down accordingly.

JS = Cosine^w1 × Jaccard^w2 × Euclidean^w3 × TFIDF^w4 × Pearson^w5

The weights (w1–w5) are normalised to sum to 1.0 before being applied. Any metric with weight 0 contributes nothing (it is treated as neutral). Two samples must score well across all weighted metrics to achieve a high joint similarity.

Pairs with a Joint Similarity at or above the threshold are drawn as edges in the network graph.

Real example — Heroin dataset: reading the four scores together

C056 vs C059 — strong candidate for common origin

Cosine0.999
Jaccard1.000
Euclidean0.979
Joint0.993

All four scores are very high and in agreement. Same 11 substances, near-identical proportions, matching profile shape. The geometric fusion produces Joint = 0.993. This pair would appear as a strong edge in the network at any threshold up to 99%.

BZ020 vs bz204 — weak overall link despite surface similarity

Cosine0.979
Jaccard0.375
Euclidean0.881
Joint0.687

Cosine alone looks compelling at 0.979, but the low Jaccard (0.375) pulls the geometric product down sharply. Joint = 0.687. The multiplicative formula penalises any weak score — if Jaccard says the substance sets are only 37.5% alike, that weakness is reflected in the final score regardless of how well the dominant components agree. This pair would appear as an edge at a threshold of 50% but not at 75%.

516 vs C054 — same substances, very different product

Cosine0.237
Jaccard1.000
Euclidean0.538

The very low Cosine (0.237) dominates here: the profile shapes are completely different even though the substance set is identical. The geometric product collapses to Joint = 0.504. A Jaccard-only analyst might flag this pair as highly similar; the joint score correctly reflects that the actual formulations are very different.

Weights & Threshold

Metric Weights

The four sliders control how much each metric contributes to the joint score. Setting a weight to zero removes that metric entirely. The weights are automatically normalised, so only their relative values matter.

SettingEffectWhen to use
Equal weights (0.25 each) Balanced contribution from all four metrics Default / exploratory analysis
High Jaccard + TF-IDF Emphasises shared rare substances Searching for common trace profiles
High Cosine + Euclidean Emphasises proportional closeness Tight batch-level comparisons

Threshold

The threshold controls which sample pairs appear as edges in the network. Only pairs with a Joint Similarity at or above this value are connected. Lowering the threshold reveals more connections; raising it shows only the strongest links.

Recommended starting point Begin with a threshold of 0.5 and adjust based on the density of the resulting network. A very dense graph (most nodes connected) suggests the threshold should be raised. A very sparse graph may benefit from a lower threshold.
Analysis Presets

Analysis Presets

Rather than manually adjusting four weight sliders, FSIS provides four named presets that configure the weights and threshold for specific investigative goals. Each preset reflects a deliberate forensic strategy — a different question you might ask of the data.

A useful starting sequence is: Source Map → Batch Match → Trace Markers. Start broad to understand the landscape, then tighten to confirm same-batch candidates, then switch to rare-substance mode to find hidden links that broad analysis missed. Use Equal Weights as a neutral reference at any point.

Source Map

Broad cluster survey — where do the families of samples lie?

Weights: Jaccard 0.55 · Cosine 0.15 · Euclidean 0.15 · TF-IDF 0.15 · Threshold 0.45

Jaccard dominates because it answers the most fundamental question in source linkage: do these two samples contain the same set of substances? A shared combination of adulterant + diluent + trace alkaloid is a much more specific fingerprint than similar proportions of a single dominant substance. Jaccard captures this regardless of concentration.

The threshold is deliberately low (0.45) to cast a wide net. At this stage you want to see the full landscape — which samples cluster together, which stand alone, and how many distinct groupings exist. It is easier to tighten later than to discover you missed a connection by starting too strict.

The other three metrics are kept at a low, equal weight rather than zero. This prevents a pair of samples that share the same substances but have wildly different proportions from scoring as highly as a pair that genuinely matches across all dimensions.

What to look for Large clusters = families of chemically related samples, likely sharing a common supply chain or laboratory. Isolated nodes (no connections) = samples with a unique substance fingerprint — potential outliers worth examining individually. Note which clusters persist as you then move to Batch Match.
Batch Match

Tight proportion matching — same recipe, same time?

Weights: Cosine 0.35 · Euclidean 0.35 · TF-IDF 0.15 · Jaccard 0.15 · Threshold 0.70

Cosine and Euclidean dominate because they enforce that both the shape of the chemical profile and the actual concentrations must agree closely. Cosine checks that the proportional ratios match — if Sample A is 60% cocaine and Sample B is 30% cocaine, their cosine will be lower even if everything else is the same. Euclidean additionally penalises the absolute proportional gap. Together they require a sample pair to look like the same product made to the same formula.

TF-IDF is kept at 0.15 rather than removed. Rare substance co-occurrence still contributes to the joint score — if two samples share an unusual trace marker as well as matching proportions, that strengthens the link. TF-IDF is simply not the primary driver here; Cosine and Euclidean do the heavy lifting. Jaccard is also kept low (0.15) because at this stage we are less interested in which substances are present and more interested in how much of each.

The high threshold (0.70) means only the very strongest matches survive. Edges that appear in this view but not in Source Map are samples that share substances but in very different proportions — possibly different stages of cutting or dilution. Edges that survive both presets are the most compelling linkage candidates.

What to look for Compare the graph here with Source Map. Clusters that shrink dramatically suggest the source-map family shares substance fingerprints but the product was not produced to a consistent formulation — different cutting ratios, different batches. Clusters that remain tight are the strongest same-batch candidates.
Trace Markers

Rare substance linking — hidden connections via unusual markers

Weights: TF-IDF 0.60 · Jaccard 0.20 · Cosine 0.10 · Euclidean 0.10 · Threshold 0.50

TF-IDF reweights every substance in inverse proportion to how commonly it appears across the dataset. A substance present in 80% of samples — such as caffeine in a heroin dataset — contributes almost nothing to the TF-IDF score. A substance present in only 5 samples contributes greatly.

The forensic rationale is that rare substances are more informative about origin. Any laboratory can add caffeine as a diluent. Far fewer will produce a sample containing a specific alkaloid impurity at trace levels — that is a marker of the raw material source or the specific synthesis route. TF-IDF amplifies exactly this signal.

As a result, two samples may appear unrelated under Source Map or Batch Match — perhaps because they look different on the surface — yet score highly here because they share a rare impurity. This can reveal connections across different apparent product types or time periods that blunt analysis misses entirely.

Cosine and Euclidean are kept low (0.10 each) rather than zero, to prevent pairs that only share a common analytical artefact from dominating the results. Jaccard at 0.20 provides a secondary anchor on the substance set.

What to look for Edges that appear here but were absent in Source Map and Batch Match. These are the most analytically interesting links — two samples that appear superficially different but share an unusual trace marker. Use the chemical filter set to Impurities alongside this preset to focus on the most forensically significant substance category.
Equal Weights

Balanced overview — no prior assumptions about what matters

Weights: All 0.25 · Threshold 0.50

Equal weights means no single aspect of the profile is privileged. A pair must score reasonably well across all four dimensions to appear as an edge. This is the most conservative of the presets in one sense — a sample pair can score highly under Source Map by virtue of Jaccard alone, but under Equal Weights it must also hold up on profile shape and proportional similarity.

Equal Weights is useful as a neutral reference: if a pair appears under Source Map but not here, it is being driven almost entirely by Jaccard — the substance sets overlap but the concentrations and profiles are quite different. If a pair appears under Batch Match but not here, the strong Cosine/Euclidean match is pulling it above threshold but Jaccard or TF-IDF are weak.

It is also the appropriate starting point when you have no prior knowledge about the dataset and do not yet know which aspect of the profiles is most variable or most informative.

What to look for Use Equal Weights as a sanity check. A pair that scores consistently across all four presets is a genuinely strong candidate for common origin. A pair that only appears under one specific preset tells you something important about what is driving the apparent similarity.
Chemical Categories

Substance Roles in Drug Samples

Each substance detected in a sample is assigned a role based on a reference dictionary. Understanding these roles helps interpret what a high similarity score actually means forensically.

Active Ingredient

The principal psychoactive substance the drug is sold as (e.g. cocaine, MDMA, heroin). Usually present in the highest proportion.

High similarity driven by the active ingredient indicates matching purity levels and is consistent with a common source, but active ingredients alone are insufficient to establish linkage — adulterants and impurities provide stronger discrimination.

Adulterant

Substances deliberately added to enhance or mimic drug effects, or to increase bulk without losing the psychoactive impression. Common examples include levamisole in cocaine and caffeine in MDMA.

Shared adulterants can be a strong indicator of common supply, particularly when the adulterant is uncommon or present in similar proportions.

Diluent / Filler

Inert or low-activity substances used to bulk out a sample and reduce costs. Examples include sugars, starch, and paracetamol.

Shared diluents have lower forensic significance than shared adulterants or impurities, as they are widely available and commonly used across many supply chains.

Precursor

Chemicals used in the synthesis of the active ingredient that remain as trace residues. Their presence can indicate the synthetic route used and potentially the source laboratory.

Shared precursors are a strong indicator of common manufacturing origin and should be prioritised in linkage analysis.

Impurity / Route Marker

By-products and degradation products formed during synthesis or storage. They are characteristic of specific synthetic routes, equipment, or storage conditions.

Route markers are highly valuable forensically because they reflect manufacturing conditions and are difficult to control deliberately. Shared impurity profiles are a strong indicator of common origin.

Analytical Artefact

Substances that arise from the analytical process itself (e.g. derivatisation agents, column bleed, instrument contaminants) rather than being genuinely present in the sample.

Artefacts have no forensic significance for linkage and should not be used to interpret similarity. Similarity driven primarily by artefacts should be treated with caution.

Investigative Methodology

Recommended Investigative Approach

Browsing the graph and following hunches has genuine value, but a structured sequence of analysis steps is more likely to surface meaningful intelligence and less likely to miss significant connections. The four workflows below represent a layered approach: start broad, confirm, then look deeper.

Two overarching goals drive the analysis:

  • Common source identification — samples with similar profiles may share a clandestine laboratory, synthesis batch, or supply chain.
  • Outlier characterisation — samples with unique profiles may represent a novel laboratory, an unusual supply route, or a one-off seizure.
Recommended sequence Workflow 1 (Map) → Workflow 2 (Confirm) → Workflow 3 (Trace) → Workflow 4 (Outliers) → Export. Each step uses the previous one as context. You can run them in any order, but this sequence is designed to move from broad intelligence to specific leads.
Threshold calibration and ROC/AUC In published forensic research (Morelato 2015; Michelot 2017), the similarity threshold is formally optimised using ROC/AUC analysis — a statistical technique that finds the threshold at which the rate of correctly identified linked pairs is maximised relative to false positives, using a set of known ground-truth links as a reference. FSIS does not implement ROC/AUC because it operates without pre-labelled data. The manual threshold you set is performing the same function intuitively: start at 0.5 (the midpoint), then move it based on whether the resulting graph looks too dense (raise it) or too sparse (lower it). Pairs that are strong intelligence leads under multiple presets are the operational equivalent of samples that score above threshold across multiple ROC-optimal metrics.
Workflow 1

Map the Landscape

Goal: understand the broad cluster structure before drilling down.

Steps

  1. Load the dataset. Apply the Source Map preset (Jaccard-heavy, threshold 0.45). This casts a wide net across the full sample set.
  2. Observe the overall shape of the graph. Note the number and size of clusters, and how many samples are isolated (no connections).
  3. Click Network Analysis and open the Community Detection tab. Run Louvain and compare its communities with the coloured clusters in the graph. Agreement between the visual layout and the algorithm-derived partition gives confidence that the groupings are real rather than a layout artefact. Where they disagree — a sample the algorithm assigns to one community but that appears visually isolated — investigate further.
  4. In the Centrality tab, review the Betweenness ranking. Samples with high betweenness sit on the path between cluster families — they are structurally bridge nodes. These are strong candidates for Workflow 4: they may represent redistribution points, transitional cutting stages, or samples that chemically straddle two supply chain arms.
  5. Switch to Equal Weights and compare. Clusters that survive both presets are more robustly linked — similarity is consistent across all metric types, not just shared substance sets.
  6. Click into the largest clusters. Use Ctrl+Click to select multiple nodes within a cluster and review the In ALL selected samples list in the drawer — these are the substances defining that cluster's shared fingerprint.
  7. Note any isolates — samples with no connections. These are candidates for Workflow 4.
What you learn The coarse supply structure of the dataset. Which samples belong to the same chemical family, how many distinct groupings exist, which samples stand apart from all others, and — via betweenness centrality — which samples are structurally important connectors between groups.
Workflow 2

Find the Definitive Links

Goal: within a cluster, confirm which pairs are likely the same batch or laboratory.

Steps

  1. Apply the Batch Match preset (Cosine + Euclidean dominant, TF-IDF secondary, threshold 0.70). Most clusters will thin — only the tightest-proportion matches survive.
  2. The remaining edges are your strongest intelligence leads: same substances and similar proportions. These pairs are candidates for same-batch or same-laboratory origin.
  3. Click Network Analysis and check the Eigenvector Centrality ranking. The top-ranked samples are the most deeply embedded in the densest cluster — connected to other highly connected samples. In a supply chain context these are the core product samples most characteristic of the primary source. Prioritise these for detailed case development.
  4. Use the chemical filter in ALL mode with the key adulterant or diluent you identified in Workflow 1. This confirms: do all linked samples in the cluster actually contain that defining substance combination?
  5. Compare which cluster edges survive Batch Match versus which were present only under Source Map. Source-Map-only edges share substances but at very different proportions — possibly different cutting stages, different batches from the same supplier, or coincidental overlap.
What you learn Which relationships within a cluster are likely same-batch versus same-lab-different-batch. Pairs that survive both Source Map and Batch Match are the most compelling linkage candidates for further investigation. Eigenvector centrality identifies the most representative samples of the dominant cluster.
Workflow 3

Hunt the Trace Markers

Goal: find hidden links through rare impurities that common-substance noise buries.

Steps

  1. Before computing, enable the √ Square Root Pre-treatment checkbox in the sidebar. This applies a square root transform to all substance concentrations before any metric is calculated, compressing dominant peaks and amplifying the relative contribution of trace impurities. Both Morelato (2015) and Michelot (2017) recommend this transform specifically for impurity-based comparison — it is the single most impactful pre-processing step for rare-marker analysis. Click Compute after enabling it.
  2. Apply the Trace Markers preset (TF-IDF dominant, threshold 0.50). Because sqrt pre-treatment has already amplified the impurity signal, you may find that the threshold can be raised slightly (e.g. to 0.55–0.60) without losing meaningful connections.
  3. Apply the chemical filter: select the Impurities category using ANY mode. This removes samples with no impurity data from the graph, focusing attention on samples where rare markers are present.
  4. Look specifically for edges that are new in this view — connections that did not appear under Source Map or Batch Match. These are pairs linked via rare substance markers rather than dominant components.
  5. Click each such pair and inspect their profiles. Identify which rare substance is the shared signal. A shared impurity at 0.5% can be more forensically probative than shared caffeine at 40%.
  6. Also look for previously isolated samples that now gain connections — these are samples whose dominant substances made them look unique, but which share a trace marker with another sample or cluster.
What you learn Subtle connections missed by broad analysis. A shared manufacturing impurity or rare alkaloid can link samples across different apparent product types, geographic areas, or time periods. These are often the most forensically significant findings.
Workflow 4

Characterise the Outliers

Goal: understand why isolated samples are unique, and whether they connect to anything at low similarity.

Steps

  1. Return to Source Map or Equal Weights. Note the samples that are isolated (no connections above the threshold).
  2. Click each isolate and inspect its chemical profile. Is it unusual because of: (a) high purity with few diluents, (b) a unique adulterant combination not seen elsewhere, or (c) simply a different expected substance from the rest of the dataset?
  3. Use the chemical filter in ALL mode: select the True Substance plus one unusual adulterant specific to that isolate. If nothing else matches, the sample is genuinely chemically unique in this dataset.
  4. Lower the threshold gradually (e.g. to 0.30). Does the isolate now connect to anything? If it connects at low threshold, examine that near-neighbour — the two samples share something, but at a lower similarity than the main clusters. This may represent a more distant supply chain relationship.
  5. Apply Trace Markers and check again — an isolate that gains a connection only under TF-IDF is linked via a rare marker, which may indicate a forensically interesting relationship not visible at the surface level.
What you learn Whether an outlier represents a genuinely novel laboratory, a unique seizure from a known supply chain at an unusual purity level, or a sample where the dominant substances happen to be different from the rest of the dataset but which shares trace markers with a known cluster.

Chemical Filter: Recommended Order of Analysis

The chemical filter restricts the graph to samples containing specific substances. The order in which you apply substance categories affects what you see and how interpretable the results are. The following order is recommended, moving from the most discriminating to the least.

OrderCategoryWhy this order
1st Adulterants Deliberately chosen by the producer — the most discriminating indicator of supply chain. Shared uncommon adulterants are a strong common-source signal.
2nd Impurities Manufacturing-specific traces that are difficult to control deliberately. Shared impurity profiles are the strongest available provenance evidence.
3rd Diluents Broad source differentiation. Common diluents (paracetamol, sugars) are too widespread to be specific, but unusual diluents can help narrow down a supply chain.
4th Substitutes Useful for identifying supply chain variation — a substitute active ingredient may indicate a different precursor source or synthesis pathway.
Last True Substance Least discriminating within a single-drug dataset — most samples contain the same active ingredient. More useful when the dataset spans multiple drug types.

ANY vs ALL mode

The filter toggle between ANY and ALL controls the logic applied when multiple substances are selected:

  • ANY (OR logic) — keeps samples containing at least one of the selected substances. Use for broad intelligence: which samples involve any of these substances?
  • ALL (AND logic) — keeps only samples containing every selected substance simultaneously. Use for definitive linkage: which samples share this exact combination?
Practical tip Start with ANY to identify the population of interest, then switch to ALL with a specific substance combination to find the tightest matches within that population. A cluster that survives ALL-mode filtering on a rare adulterant + impurity combination is a very strong common-source candidate.
Workflow 5

Export and Further Analysis

Goal: formalise findings and take them into dedicated tools for reporting or deeper network analysis.

Once the four analytical workflows are complete and the most significant clusters, links, and outliers have been identified, the current graph can be exported for further work outside FSIS.

Steps

  1. Set the final graph state you want to export: choose the preset (or custom weights), threshold, and any active chemical or year filters that best represent your analytical conclusions.
  2. Click Export GraphML. This downloads a .graphml file containing every node (sample) and edge (link above threshold), with centrality scores, metadata, and joint similarity values pre-attached as attributes.
  3. Open the file in Gephi for publication-quality layout and visual reporting. Run ForceAtlas 2 to separate clusters, colour nodes by a metadata field (e.g. expected substance or seizure year), and scale edge thickness by joint_similarity. Export as SVG or PDF for inclusion in intelligence reports.
  4. For quantitative reporting, load the file in Python/NetworkX: G = nx.read_graphml("file.graphml"). From here you can run additional statistical analysis — for example Spearman correlation between centrality scores and metadata fields, or hierarchical clustering on the joint similarity matrix — that goes beyond what FSIS provides directly.
  5. Consider running separate exports for each preset (Source Map, Batch Match, Trace Markers) and comparing them in Gephi as separate graph layers. Edges that appear in all three exports are the most robustly supported links in the dataset — the operational equivalent of surviving multiple similarity metrics in ROC/AUC evaluation.
What you learn An exportable, attributed graph that captures the full analytical state at the moment of export. The centrality scores pre-computed in the file provide an immediate ranking of sample importance without needing to re-run the analysis in an external tool. Comparing exports across presets in a tool like Gephi provides a rigorous multi-metric view of linkage confidence.
Note on statistical thresholds Published forensic studies (Morelato 2015; Michelot 2017) formally determine optimal similarity thresholds using ROC/AUC on labelled datasets where links between seizures are known. If you have access to confirmed case links (e.g. seizures known to be from the same laboratory), you can use those as a reference to evaluate whether the FSIS threshold and weights correctly identify them. Pairs that score above threshold across multiple presets and survive the Trace Markers workflow represent the closest operational equivalent to a formally validated link.

Using the App

Network Analysis

The Network Analysis button (below the graph statistics in the sidebar) opens a panel that runs two classes of computation on the current graph: centrality measures and community detection. Both operate on whatever graph is currently displayed — the active threshold, weight settings, and chemical/year filters all apply.

Centrality Measures

Centrality scores rank samples by their structural importance within the similarity network. Three measures are computed simultaneously.

Degree Centrality

How many samples is this one connected to?

Degree centrality is the proportion of all other samples in the graph to which a given sample is directly connected (has an edge above the threshold). A score of 1.0 would mean a sample is connected to every other sample.

Forensic interpretation: high-degree samples are chemically similar to many others — consistent with widely distributed product from a central supply chain. A sample with unusually high degree in a Trace Markers view warrants particular attention: it shares rare impurities with many others.

Betweenness Centrality

Does this sample sit on the path between clusters?

Betweenness centrality measures how often a sample lies on the shortest path between every other pair of samples in the network. A sample with high betweenness is a bridge node — it links otherwise separate parts of the graph.

Forensic interpretation: a bridge sample may represent a transitional product — something at an intermediate cutting stage, a redistribution point in the supply chain, or a sample that chemically connects two otherwise distinct laboratory clusters. These are often the most analytically interesting samples to examine in detail.

Eigenvector Centrality

Is this sample connected to other well-connected samples?

Eigenvector centrality extends degree centrality by weighting a sample's connections by how central those neighbours are. Being connected to high-degree samples scores higher than being connected to isolated ones. This is the same principle used by PageRank in web search.

Forensic interpretation: samples with high eigenvector centrality sit at the heart of the most densely connected part of the network. In a supply chain context, these are the samples most deeply embedded in the dominant cluster — candidates for the core product from the primary source in the dataset.

Community Detection

Community detection algorithms partition the graph into groups (communities) of samples that are more densely connected to each other than to the rest of the network. Four algorithms are available, each using a different strategy. Results are presented in tabs; the modularity score shown for each algorithm measures how well-separated the communities are (0 = no structure, 1 = perfect separation — values above 0.3 are generally considered meaningful).

Algorithm Approach When it is most useful
Louvain Iteratively merges nodes to maximise modularity. Fast and widely used. General-purpose starting point. Deterministic (seeded) in FSIS, so results are reproducible.
Greedy Modularity Agglomerative — starts with each node as its own community and merges greedily. Often produces fewer, larger communities than Louvain. Good for identifying the coarsest supply chain structure.
Label Propagation Each node adopts the majority label of its neighbours, iterated to convergence. Very fast. Stochastic — results may vary slightly between runs. Compare with Louvain as a sanity check.
Girvan-Newman Removes the edge with the highest betweenness centrality first, then repeats. Deterministic. Only runs on graphs with 200 or fewer nodes (performance limit). Best for finding the single most important split in a smaller dataset.
Practical tip Community detection groups are not the same as the coloured connected components in the main graph — those reflect direct connectivity above the threshold, while communities reflect denser-than-average internal linkage, which may cross component boundaries at low thresholds. Compare Louvain communities with the visual clusters in the graph: disagreements are analytically interesting.
Graph dependency Network Analysis operates on the current graph state. Changing the threshold, weights, or chemical filter and recomputing will change the graph — and therefore the centrality rankings and community assignments. Always note which settings were active when interpreting results.

Analytics

The Analytics button opens a four-tab panel that provides statistical context beyond the main network graph. All four tabs operate on the current graph state — the dataset, threshold, metric weights, chemical filter, and year range that were active when you last clicked Compute. Clicking Analytics before Compute will show an empty or stale view; always compute first.

Filter-aware If you have a chemical filter active and click Compute, the Analytics results reflect only the filtered sample set. This lets you compare subsets of your data in isolation — for example, isolating samples that contain a particular cutting agent and then examining how that cohort clusters over time.

Metric Comparison

The Metric Comparison tab runs each similarity metric in isolation — and then the joint equal-weight combination — at the current threshold and reports the resulting graph structure. This tells you how sensitive your conclusions are to the choice of metric.

What the table shows

ColumnDescription
ConfigurationThe metric being tested (Cosine only, Jaccard only, Euclidean only, TF-IDF only, Pearson only, Joint equal weights). The row matching your current joint settings is highlighted.
EdgesNumber of pairs above the threshold under this metric.
ClustersNumber of connected components with more than one node.
IsolatesSamples with no connections above the threshold — the "loners" in the network.
Largest cluster %The fraction of all samples that fall into the single biggest cluster. High values indicate a dominant supercluster.
Mean similarityAverage pairwise similarity across all pairs — reflects the overall signal density.

How to interpret it

Look for agreement and disagreement between metrics:

  • Metrics agree on cluster structure — the finding is robust; it does not depend on which mathematical lens you apply.
  • One metric stands apart — that metric is detecting a signal the others miss. TF-IDF standing apart often means rare-substance links are present that proportion-based metrics smooth over. Jaccard standing apart often means shared presence/absence of substances (regardless of amount) is the dominant signal.
  • Pearson differs from the rest — Pearson is sensitive to relative concentration patterns, not just shared substances. Disagreement suggests that some pairs share the same substances but in systematically different proportions.
Use before finalising weights Run Metric Comparison at your analysis threshold before locking in your weight settings. If one metric is producing a very different cluster count, consider whether its signal is analytically meaningful for your question, and weight accordingly.

Threshold Sweep

The Threshold Sweep tab plots how graph structure changes as the threshold moves from 0.45 to 0.90, using your current metric weights. A second line shows the same sweep under equal weights for reference. The current threshold is marked with a red dashed vertical line.

Reading the chart

The chart has two y-axes:

  • Left axis (blue line) — percentage of all samples in the largest cluster. Falling steeply as threshold rises indicates the dataset has one dominant super-cluster at low thresholds that fragments at higher ones.
  • Right axis (orange line) — number of distinct clusters. A peak in cluster count at intermediate thresholds is the classic "sweet spot" — enough resolution to separate groups, not so strict that everything fragments into isolates.
  • Grey lines — same metrics under equal weights. Divergence between blue and grey lines shows how much your weight choices are shaping the result.

Choosing a threshold

There is no universally correct threshold. Practical guidance:

  • Set the threshold at the point where the cluster-count curve peaks — this maximises discrimination before fragmentation.
  • If the largest-cluster line drops sharply between two thresholds, that gap is where the dominant supercluster breaks apart. Comparing the graph just before and after that threshold reveals which samples are the bridging links.
  • If the cluster count never rises (monotonically decreasing), the data does not support clear discrete groupings — investigate whether a chemical filter would reveal subgroup structure.
Sweep uses the current joint similarity matrix The sweep varies the threshold, not the weights. It does not recompute the joint similarity at each step — it re-applies the current matrix to each threshold. This makes it fast, but it means the sweep result reflects the metric weights that were active at Compute time, not equal weights for the equal-weight line. The equal-weight line is computed from the equal-weight joint matrix computed during the sweep.

Cluster Profiles

The Cluster Profiles tab generates a chemical fingerprint for each cluster in the current graph — showing which substances are most prevalent, how internally similar the cluster is, and when the samples were collected. Isolates (samples with no connections above the threshold) are listed separately.

Cluster cards

Each cluster card shows:

  • Sample count and within-cluster similarity — the mean pairwise joint similarity among all members. High values (>0.80) indicate a tight, chemically coherent group. Low values (<0.50) suggest the cluster is held together by a few bridging substances rather than an overall similar profile.
  • Top substances with proportion bars — each bar shows the fraction of cluster samples in which that substance appears (prevalence), not the concentration. A substance at 100% appears in every sample in the cluster; one at 20% appears in only a fifth.
  • Year distribution — shows which calendar years are represented in the cluster. A cluster spanning many years suggests a stable supply chain pattern; a cluster concentrated in a single year may reflect a batch or a short-lived supplier.

Isolate cards

Isolates are samples that have no connections above the current threshold. They appear individually with their top substances. Common reasons for isolation:

  • The sample has an unusual chemical composition that does not match any other sample in the filtered dataset.
  • The sample may be from a different drug category than expected — check the substance list for unexpected primary substances.
  • The threshold is too high — lower it to see whether the isolate connects at a more permissive threshold.
Cluster numbering Clusters are numbered in descending size order — Cluster 1 is always the largest. Numbering changes when you recompute with different settings. Do not use cluster numbers as stable identifiers across analyses; describe clusters by their chemical composition instead.

Timeline

The Timeline tab breaks the dataset down by year of collection, reporting within-year similarity, cluster structure, and cross-year similarity. A stacked bar chart shows how substance composition shifts across years. This tab is most informative when your dataset spans multiple years.

Year statistics table

ColumnDescription
YearCalendar year of sample collection (from the dataset's date field).
SamplesNumber of samples collected in that year.
Mean similarityAverage pairwise joint similarity among all samples collected in that year. Rising values over time indicate increasing homogeneity within the supply for that period.
Std devStandard deviation of pairwise similarities within the year. High std dev means the cohort is internally diverse — some pairs are very similar, others very different.

Cross-year similarity matrix

The cross-year table shows the mean joint similarity between every pair of years. Each cell is the average similarity of all pairs where one sample is from year A and the other is from year B. Diagonal cells are the within-year means.

A high off-diagonal value between two years indicates that the supply profile in those years was chemically similar — the same adulterant or cutting-agent pattern was present. A sharp drop between consecutive years can signal a supply disruption or source change.

Substance composition chart

The stacked bar chart shows the top substances (by overall prevalence) and their year-by-year prevalence. Each bar segment is the fraction of samples in that year that contain the substance. This makes visible trends such as:

  • A cutting agent rising over time — a new adulterant entering the supply chain.
  • The primary substance declining — falling purity; the active substance is a smaller fraction of total composition year-on-year.
  • A substance appearing briefly then disappearing — a short-lived batch or supplier.
Interpreting homogeneity changes Rising within-year mean similarity does not necessarily mean the supply is "better." It often means samples are converging on a common profile — frequently because purity is falling and adulterant mixtures are becoming more uniform. Always read the composition chart alongside the similarity statistics to determine whether homogeneity reflects consistent quality or consistent adulteration.
Small year cohorts Years with fewer than five samples produce unreliable mean-similarity and cluster statistics. The cross-year similarity will also be noisy when one year has very few samples. Treat single-year or two-sample cohorts as indicative only.
